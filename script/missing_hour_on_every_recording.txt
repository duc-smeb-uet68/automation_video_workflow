This is a fictional story.

At 2:14 in the morning, the camera outside the Bellwether grocery store showed an empty street.

At 3:14, the same camera showed forty-seven people standing in the rain.

The recording between those moments was gone.

So were the recordings from the bank, the gas station, the school, and the police station. A baby monitor had captured a child's breathing, then silence, then the child crying. A delivery driver's dashboard camera had lost the same hour. Even the weather station's automatic log had stopped and started again.

The people in the street were facing the old railway tunnel at the edge of town. Some wore coats. Some wore pajamas. One woman had brought a birthday cake with seven unlit candles.

None of them could explain why.

By sunrise, the story had reached Mara Vale, a digital evidence examiner who had grown up in Bellwether and spent twenty-three years trying not to return.

The call came from her younger brother, Daniel.

He told her their mother had been among the people in the street. She was safe. She was home. But she had come back carrying a small red shoe, and she would not say where she had found it.

Mara recognized the shoe before he finished describing it.

It had belonged to their sister, June.

June disappeared when she was seven years old. The shoe had disappeared with her.

Mara arrived that afternoon to find the town divided between people who wanted an explanation and people who wanted everyone to stop looking for one.

Her mother belonged to the second group.

She was sitting at the kitchen table with the shoe in front of her. The leather was wet, and there was pale grit packed into its seams. She turned it over and over in her hands, as though testing its weight.

“Where did you get it?” Mara asked.

Her mother looked at the window. “Someone gave it to me.”

“Who?”

“I don't remember.”

Then she said something stranger.

“I think I promised to bring it back.”

Mara took the shoe to the police station. She did not clean it. She photographed the grit before a technician collected it. Then she asked to see the original recordings, the devices, and the records of who had handled them.

She knew a gap on a screen was not necessarily a gap in time. A recorder could fail. A file could be overwritten. A clock could be wrong.

In some places, clocks jump forward when daylight saving time begins. An hour disappears from the local clock face, but people do not disappear with it. One fifty-nine becomes three o'clock because the label changes, not because the world stops.

Bellwether's gap was in October. The timestamps skipped from 2:14 to 3:14. More importantly, people and objects had moved between the last frame and the first. The delivery driver had entered town with a full tank and left it with less fuel. The birthday cake had lost a slice.

A changed clock could not explain that.

It could not explain why several cameras had stopped writing data either. Some used a shared recording system. Others stored footage locally. The baby monitor saved to a card. The dashboard camera recorded to another. Investigators would need to examine each device before deciding what had happened; similar-looking gaps did not prove a common cause.

The power company reported no town-wide outage. A technician found no obvious fault on the bank's recorder. That still left plenty of possibilities: a local interruption, damaged storage, deliberate deletion, an error nobody had noticed.

Mara made a list of every possibility she could test.

She also made a list of what people said they remembered. She wrote each account separately, before showing anyone else's version.

The baker remembered waking on the street with icing on her sleeve. The school caretaker remembered walking toward the tunnel, although he could not say when he had left home. The delivery driver remembered a woman in a yellow coat waving him down. He was certain he had stopped.

But the last image before his camera's gap showed nobody on the road.

One account bothered Mara more than the rest.

An elderly neighbor, Mrs. Harker, said she had seen the crowd from her upstairs window. They stood quietly in two lines, leaving a passage down the middle. Several people held objects: a coat, a blanket, a lantern.

“As if they were expecting someone,” Mrs. Harker said.

“Did you see anyone arrive?”

Mrs. Harker hesitated. “I think so.”

“Who?”

“A little girl.”

Mara kept her face still.

“What was she wearing?”

“I can't remember.”

“How old was she?”

“I'm not sure.”

Only later did Mrs. Harker mention the red shoe. By then, Daniel had told three neighbors about it.

That did not mean Mrs. Harker was lying. Memory can be shaped by what people hear afterward. A detail offered in a conversation can become part of the picture someone believes they saw. Certainty is not the same as proof.

Mara wanted to protect the witness from her own questions. She wanted, even more, to ask whether the girl had looked like June.

She did not ask.

That evening Daniel took her to their mother's attic. He found a box of June's things: a paper crown, a marble in a sock, and a photograph of three children standing in front of the railway tunnel.

Mara was eleven in the photograph. Daniel was nine. June stood between them, holding a yellow raincoat over her head like a roof.

Behind the children, someone had written a date on the tunnel wall in white paint.

The date was October nineteenth.

June vanished on October twentieth.

Mara remembered the night in pieces. The sound of rain on their roof. Their mother calling June's name. The search party's lanterns moving among the trees. She remembered the tunnel too, but had never been certain whether she remembered going there or only remembered the adults discussing it afterward.

Daniel had a different memory. He said their mother had made them wait at home.

Mara showed him the photograph.

“That was the afternoon before,” he said. “We went looking for the train tracks.”

“Do you remember who took it?”

He shook his head.

The photograph had been developed years ago. There was no reliable time attached to it. It placed the children at the tunnel around the time June disappeared, but it could not tell Mara where they had been that night.

At the bottom of the box she found a folded newspaper clipping. It reported June's disappearance and described the search. On the back was an advertisement for a wedding photographer. Someone had circled the telephone number.

Mara called it. The number no longer worked.

The photographer, Elias Dunn, was still alive. Mrs. Harker knew where he lived: in a house beyond the old rail line, a place with a greenhouse and a roof that leaned like a tired shoulder.

Elias opened the door before Mara knocked. He held a small metal tin.

“Your brother told me you were back,” he said. “I thought someone might finally ask.”

Inside the tin was a strip of film and an envelope. The envelope contained a note in June's childish handwriting.

If I get lost, wait for me with the light.

Mara read it twice. She remembered June making up games about getting lost and found. She remembered the girl asking her to stand very still while she hid in the garden. But she could not remember this note.

“She gave it to me the day she disappeared,” Elias said. “I was fixing her mother's camera. June asked me to keep it safe.”

“Why didn't you give it to the police?”

“I did tell them. I showed them the note. They didn't think it told them where she went.”

He had made a copy before handing it over. Mara checked the old case file later. A note matching his description was listed among the items collected, though the copy in the file was difficult to read.

Elias pointed to the film strip. “That came from my garden camera. It was running last night.”

He explained that he used an old film camera to photograph foxes near the railway. Its automatic winding mechanism had been unreliable for years. He had set it facing the tunnel road and left it alone. It was not connected to the town's recording system, and it did not print a clock time onto each frame.

Mara asked if she could examine the camera and the rest of the film. Elias handed her the tin, the camera, and the spool he had removed that morning. She photographed everything and wrote down what he told her. The film had passed through his hands before she saw it, so its history could not be established by her examination alone.

The first images showed a fox near the garden fence. Then came a stretch of darkness and blurred rain. After that, the road appeared. People were walking toward the tunnel.

Some carried things. A lantern. A folded blanket. A cake box.

There was Mara's mother, her hair uncovered, holding the red shoe.

Then there was a frame of the crowd standing in two lines.

Mara felt her hands tighten on the edge of the table. It looked like Mrs. Harker's account. But Mrs. Harker had spoken to other people before Mara interviewed her, and Elias had handled the film. The agreement was a clue, not an answer.

On the next frame, the people had turned toward the tunnel.

At the far end of the passage stood a small figure in a yellow coat.

The image was too blurred to show a face.

Elias had heard the winding mechanism struggle during the night. After that frame, several exposures overlapped. Figures and light smeared across the film. The next clear image showed the crowd breaking apart.

Mara's mother was walking away from the tunnel. Her hands were empty.

But she had brought the shoe home.

Mara examined the remaining frames twice. She could not tell when her mother had picked it up again, or whether the shoe in the photograph was the same one she carried home. The images did not show what happened inside the tunnel. They did not prove how long the crowd had stood there.

The laboratory reported that the shoe contained railway ballast dust, soil, and traces of old wood. It was consistent with having been near the tunnel. The technician could not say when it had been there.

By then, word of the film had spread. People who had remembered nothing began recalling a yellow coat. One man insisted he had spoken to the child. A woman claimed she had heard a train, though no train had used the line for decades.

Daniel wanted to show the image to their mother. Mara asked him to wait until she had recorded her account again.

Their mother listened as Mara described the film without mentioning the figure. For a while she said nothing. Then she rose, went to the cupboard, and brought down the birthday candles from the kitchen drawer.

There were six.

“I thought I had seven,” she said.

The baker had brought seven candles to the street. One had vanished with the missing slice of cake. Mara asked if her mother remembered taking it.

“No.”

“Do you remember the tunnel?”

“I remember the rain.”

“Do you remember anyone there?”

Her mother looked at her for a long time.

“I remember waiting,” she said. “I remember thinking that if I turned around, she would be gone.”

Mara asked who she meant.

Her mother pressed the six candles into Mara's hand. “If I tell you, you'll think I saw her.”

“Did you?”

“I don't know.”

That night Mara went to the tunnel with Daniel. The police had searched it that morning. They had found no sign of a child and no evidence that anyone had been living inside.

The opening was low, its brickwork dark with water. Their flashlight beams crossed the tracks and settled on the old white paint. Most of the date from the photograph had flaked away.

Daniel stopped beside it.

“I remember this,” he said.

He touched a shallow crack between two bricks. Inside was a small hollow, just large enough for a child's hand.

Mara knelt. There was something wrapped in oilcloth at the back. Daniel lifted it out: a photograph, folded into quarters and stained at the edges.

It showed the three children in front of the tunnel. June held the yellow coat over her head. This was another print of the photograph from the attic, but someone had written on the back.

Mara recognized her own childhood handwriting.

Wait here. I will come back with the light.

She remembered then, or thought she did. June had hidden the picture in the wall during their game. Mara had gone home for a lantern. When she returned, June was gone.

For years she had remembered the search, the rain, the adults. She had not remembered leaving her sister there.

Daniel read the words over her shoulder. He said he remembered going home with her. Their father had stopped them at the door and asked why they were wet. They had fetched the lantern together.

“We weren't gone long,” he said.

Neither of them could know exactly how long. They had been children, and they had discussed that night many times since. Mara wrote down what each of them recalled before they spoke again. She folded the photograph carefully and put it in an evidence bag.

Outside, the town was dark. The power had failed on their street, though lights still burned in houses farther up the hill. Someone had set a lantern on the road facing the tunnel.

Beside it sat the cake box, folded flat to keep off the rain.

Mara lifted the lid. Inside was one unlit birthday candle and a piece of paper torn from a school notebook.

The writing was uneven, as if made by someone learning to form the letters.

Thank you for waiting. Please don't bring the shoe back.

Daniel looked up the road. No one was there.

Mara checked the paper and the candle without touching them again. She called the police, photographed the box where it lay, and stayed beside it until an officer arrived. Someone could have left it after hearing about the film, the cake, and the shoe. Someone could have copied a child's handwriting. The note was another question, not an answer.

Their mother read it the next morning. She put the red shoe in June's old box and closed the lid.

The town's recordings remained incomplete. Technicians found faults on two of the devices, but they could not establish one cause for all the gaps. The film showed people waiting near the tunnel and a small figure in a yellow coat. It did not show who the figure was.

The police kept the note and the photograph. They reopened June's file, interviewed Elias, and asked anyone who had been near the tunnel to come forward.

Mara stayed in Bellwether for another week. Each evening she sat with her mother at the kitchen table. Sometimes Daniel joined them. They talked about June's games, about the coat, about things they remembered and things they only thought they remembered. When their accounts differed, they let them differ.

On her last morning, Mara went to say goodbye at the railway tunnel. The lantern had been taken away. Rain had filled the narrow hollow between the bricks.

She stood there for a minute, holding the photograph in her mind: three children under a yellow coat, all of them looking toward whoever held the camera.

She still did not know who had taken the picture. She did not know who had left the note. And she could not tell whether the figure on the film was June.

But when she turned to leave, she saw that someone had placed a second lantern beside the first lantern's dry mark on the road.

It was lit.
