# The Village With One Extra Resident: Research and Editorial Notes

Research date: 30 October 2026.

Companion narration: `the_village_with_one_extra_resident.txt`.

## Status of the story

This is an original work of historical supernatural fiction. The title and initial premise were proposed as fiction in the conversation. Bellwether, Clara Venn, Nell Harrow, Mrs. Bale, May, the clerk, the schoolmistress, their dialogue, the population discrepancies, the relief account, the slate, the death, and the supernatural incidents are invented. No source below documents this village or case. The opening sentence of the narration explicitly identifies its fictional status.

The research supports the historical setting and examines how people distinguish physical presence, belonging, hospitality, and remembrance. It does not authenticate a haunting. The narrative follows an invented investigation rather than reporting an actual archival discovery.

## Questions investigated

1. How did the 1921 census of England and Wales define presence, and what did enumerators collect?
2. What records might preserve a child's identity outside a census household entry?
3. What documented traditions give an empty place a meaning beyond a missing diner?
4. How have literature and collected folklore treated people who remain part of a household despite physical absence?
5. How can those sources inform a mystery without being misrepresented as evidence for its events?

## Sources and their use

### 1. The National Archives: Census records

[Census records: research guide](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/census-records/). Institutional source. Relevant sections on procedure, dates, household schedules, enumeration districts, and missing material were read.

The guide places the 1921 census on Sunday, 19 June. It explains that enumerators distributed household schedules, householders recorded people who slept in the dwelling on census night, and enumerators collected the forms afterward. It also distinguishes the preserved household schedules of 1911 and 1921 from earlier enumeration books. For 1921, there are Plans of Division rather than the enumerators' summary books used in 1911.

Narrative use: the date, overnight-presence rule, collection after census night, and Clara's distinction between a living resident and a remembered dead person. The historical procedure motivates her initial search for a lodger, visitor, or addition error.

Boundary: the clerk's tally and inherited notebooks are explicitly private parish papers invented for the story. They are not represented as an authentic 1921 census summary book or a discovered National Archives record. Clara does not add a ghost to the official household schedules. The invented earlier discrepancy totals are not historical statistics. The guide also establishes 3 April as the 1881 census date; the rain, flood, and events assigned to that night are entirely fictional.

### 2. The National Archives: Children's homes

[Children's homes: research guide](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/children-care/). Institutional source. Relevant sections on individual records, local archives, and provision from 1834 to 1930 were read.

The guide describes several forms of care for impoverished children, including boarding out with local families. It explains that records of individual children are more likely to survive with an institution or local authority archive than in central government holdings. Admission, discharge, and other local records can therefore matter when reconstructing a child's life.

Narrative use: looking beyond a household list, and the broad historical possibility of a child living with a family outside the child's birth family. The plot gives Clara an invented local relief account that records work, assistance, and disputed addresses.

Boundary: the guide does not document Nell, her placement, the fictional payments, the village's conduct, or any connection between boarding out and a census omission. Nell's later exploitation is the conduct of invented individuals. The story makes no claim that such treatment was legally authorized, routine, or universal. The relief account is a fictional clue, not a quoted historical record.

### 3. Culture.pl, Adam Mickiewicz Institute: The empty chair

Wojciech Oleksiak, [Why Do Poles Leave One Chair Empty on Christmas Eve?](https://culture.pl/en/article/why-do-poles-leave-one-chair-empty-on-christmas-eve). Cultural institution's interpretive article. The article was read, including its discussion of alternative origins.

The article describes a Polish Christmas Eve custom of preparing an additional chair and place setting for an unexpected visitor. It discusses meanings associated with hospitality, deceased relatives, and absent people. It presents several possible historical explanations rather than establishing one uncontested origin.

Narrative use: an empty place can preserve an obligation to welcome somebody. In the story, a gesture of remembrance gradually becomes a fear-driven routine; May changes its meaning by offering her own place near the fire.

Boundary: this is a Polish Christmas Eve tradition. It is not evidence that English villages laid an extra place every day or shared Bellwether's invented practice. The story borrows the broad visual and ethical motif, not the custom's cultural identity. The article's proposed ancient origins, biblical explanation, and later historical developments are not asserted in the narration. A real hospitality tradition is not presented as sinister in itself.

### 4. William Wordsworth: We Are Seven

[We Are Seven](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/52298/we-are-seven), original poem reproduced by the Poetry Foundation. The poem itself was read.

The speaker asks a child how many siblings there are and tries to subtract two who have died. The child continues to include them in the family. Her visits to their graves and her meals near them make remembrance an ongoing relationship rather than a failure to perform arithmetic.

Narrative use: the central distinction between counting people physically present and recognizing who belongs. Clara's memory of her brother gives her a personal reason to understand both kinds of counting. May's response is expressed through an offered chair rather than an argument about a number.

Boundary: this is literary evidence for a theme, not an empirical account of ghosts, a psychological diagnosis, or proof that dead people should be listed as living census residents. The script does not quote the poem or reproduce its characters and events.

### 5. Thomas Keightley: The Brownie, in The Fairy Mythology

[The Brownie](https://www.sacred-texts.com/neu/celt/tfm/tfm130.htm), historical folklore collection reproduced by the Internet Sacred Text Archive. The chapter was read directly.

Keightley's collected accounts describe a household spirit associated with domestic work, discreet food offerings, attachment to a household, and departure after particular kinds of offense. The chapter contains variations and conflicting versions, rather than a single fixed set of supernatural rules.

Narrative use: the unsettling familiarity of food left near a hearth and an unseen visitor treated as part of domestic routine. This makes the early clues open to several explanations before the human history emerges.

Boundary: a historical folklore collection records stories and beliefs; it does not verify supernatural beings. The script does not identify Nell as a brownie. Her death, the knocking, the wet marks, the census discrepancy, and the resolution are original inventions. Keightley's broad comparative claims and obsolete terminology are not adopted as modern scholarship. No food-offering rule is represented as universal across cultures.

## Evidence synthesis and narrative decisions

- The official census source supplies a precise administrative question: who was present on a particular night? The story's moral question concerns responsibility for someone whom the community failed to house. The two questions are deliberately kept separate.
- The child's identity emerges through several invented records and witness accounts, rather than being inferred solely from an empty chair. The slate provides a visual clue; the relief account supplies a name; Mrs. Bale supplies a fallible personal memory.
- The discrepancy remains one person across changing totals. The story does not require a village to retain exactly 143 living residents through decades of births, deaths, and departures.
- Mrs. Bale's account is fictional witness testimony within the story. The narrator does not present it as an independently corroborated historical finding. Clara records further testimony and signatures instead of treating a confession or a corrected sum as complete accountability.
- The physical explanations for the chair, consumed food, knocking, and water are not eliminated. The ending preserves supernatural uncertainty while resolving the human question of Nell's name and treatment. That ambiguity is a storytelling decision, not a claim about real evidence.
- Nell's apparent haunting is not a mechanism that kills, replaces, or threatens villagers. The mystery rests on an ignored request for shelter and the difference between a ritual place and an offered home.
- The narration uses original prose and invented dialogue. No sourced quotation, reconstructed real conversation, or purported historical document is presented as authentic.
- Source links remain in this companion file so the narration contains no spoken URLs, citation markers, section labels, or production directions. The fictional-status disclosure remains part of the narration.

## Research limits

Five relevant source texts were inspected. The archive guides are modern descriptions of historical records, not inspection of original household schedules or individual care files. The Culture.pl article is an interpretive cultural account; the poem and folklore chapter are primary texts for literary and folkloric motifs, not evidence of supernatural events.

A linked National Archives blog page about people missing from censuses could not be retrieved. A follow-up search did not provide an accessible text version, so no specific claim relies on that page. A targeted search for a documented ghost-census case did not return a relevant authenticated case; that search is not proof that no such legend exists. It provides no basis for presenting the invented premise as nonfiction.

The research does not establish that Clara's exact assignment, the parish book, the relief account's format, or the village gathering followed a documented administrative procedure. Those connective details are fiction. No inference about a real village, family, or identifiable historical child is intended.
