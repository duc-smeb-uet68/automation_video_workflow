In 1845, two British ships sailed toward the Arctic, carrying 129 men and the hopes of an empire. Their mission was to find a sea route through the ice. None of those men would return.

For years, searchers found no ships and no survivors. Then they began to uncover fragments: graves on a lonely island, a message hidden beneath stones, and stories passed down by Inuit families. More than a century and a half later, the ships themselves emerged from the darkness. But finding them did not end the mystery. It gave investigators new questions to ask.

This is the story of HMS Erebus and HMS Terror, and the people who helped bring their story back to the surface.

The expedition left England in May 1845 under Sir John Franklin, a veteran naval officer and Arctic explorer. Britain wanted to chart the Northwest Passage, a sea route connecting the Atlantic and Pacific through the Arctic archipelago. Europeans had searched for such a passage for centuries. Ice, distance, and a brief summer made it a formidable challenge.

Erebus and Terror were strong ships, adapted for polar travel. Their bows were reinforced, and each carried a steam engine and a propeller to help when wind failed. They carried provisions for years, as well as scientific instruments and books. Their crews included officers, sailors, and Royal Marines.

Yet equipment could not guarantee an escape route. The sea ahead was not an empty space on a map. It was a changing landscape of channels and ice, a homeland where Inuit communities had lived and travelled for generations. The expedition would enter a world whose conditions its officers could not control.

After stopping in Greenland, the ships were seen by European whalers in Baffin Bay in the summer of 1845. Those were the last confirmed European sightings of the expedition. When no news came, concern in Britain grew. Search parties would spend years trying to follow the ships into the Arctic.

One of the first places to reveal a clue was Beechey Island. There, the expedition had spent its first Arctic winter. Searchers found three graves, belonging to men who died in 1846. The ships had continued south, but the graves showed that the voyage had already claimed lives.

For a long time, the trail seemed to vanish into the ice. Then, in 1859, a search party on King William Island found a metal container beneath a pile of stones. Inside was a naval form with two handwritten messages. Today, it is known as the Victory Point note.

The first message was dated May 1847. It recorded the expedition's movements and said that Franklin was in command. Its brief assessment was reassuring: all was well.

Around the edge of that same paper, a second message told a different story. It was dated April 25, 1848. Erebus and Terror, it said, had been trapped in ice since September 1846. The crews had abandoned the ships three days before the second message was written.

Franklin had died on June 11, 1847. By the time the note was completed, 24 members of the expedition had died. The 105 men still alive were under Captain Francis Crozier's command. The note said they planned to set out the next day for the Back River, on the mainland.

That is what the paper tells us. It does not explain why Franklin died, why so many others had died, or exactly what happened after the men left the ships. The note records a plan to march south. It cannot tell us how far each man travelled, whether every group followed the same route, or whether some returned to a ship.

Other evidence would come from people who knew this land intimately. Inuit encountered members of the expedition and later described what they had seen. Their accounts, preserved in oral history and recorded by searchers, included encounters with struggling men and observations of abandoned equipment and human remains.

In 1854, explorer John Rae reported information he had gathered from Inuit, including evidence that some of the starving men had resorted to cannibalism. His report caused outrage in Britain. Some refused to accept that naval crews could have faced such a desperate end.

Later archaeological work found cut marks on human bones from expedition sites. These findings support the accounts of cannibalism. They do not reveal every person's final moments, and they should not be mistaken for evidence of murder. They point to a catastrophe in which some survivors faced extreme hunger.

Inuit testimony has been essential, but it does not form a single simple timeline. Accounts describe encounters and discoveries in different places, sometimes recorded years later by outsiders. Researchers still debate how to fit them together with the Victory Point note and archaeological evidence. What is clear is that Inuit knowledge preserved important information while British searchers struggled to reconstruct the expedition's fate.

Why did the expedition fail? There is no single proven answer. The ships were immobilized for a long period. Men died before the decision to abandon them. Those who left faced cold, exhaustion, and a long journey with limited means of support. Illness and nutritional deficiencies may also have weakened them.

Lead poisoning has often been suggested as an explanation. Researchers have found evidence of lead exposure in remains associated with the expedition, but the degree to which it contributed to the disaster is disputed. It is not established as the single cause. The available evidence points to a complicated sequence, not an easy solution.

Even the ships' final journeys remain uncertain. The Victory Point note says they were abandoned in April 1848, but their wrecks were eventually found far from that recorded position. Ice can move ships. Some researchers also think survivors may have returned and managed to move at least one vessel. The evidence has not settled exactly how either ship reached its final resting place.

For generations, the locations of the wrecks remained a puzzle to outside searchers. Inuit oral histories kept knowledge of the ships and their surroundings alive. Inuk historian Louie Kamookak gathered accounts from elders and worked to bring that knowledge into the search. Modern expeditions combined historical research, Inuit knowledge, and underwater technology.

In 2014, a Parks Canada-led expedition found Erebus south of King William Island. Two years later, the Arctic Research Foundation found Terror in Terror Bay. Inuk hunter Sammy Kogvik had told a fellow crew member about a piece of wood he had seen protruding from the ice there. Divers later verified the wreck's identity by examining features that matched the ship's design.

Terror lay in remarkably good condition, its structure preserved in the cold Arctic water. For investigators, the discovery was extraordinary. Here was a ship that had disappeared from European view generations earlier, still holding evidence of the people who had sailed aboard it.

Archaeologists have explored the wrecks and recovered objects connected to daily life, work, and science. Each object has a location and a context. Together, they can help researchers understand how the ships were used and what happened aboard them. But an object cannot always answer the question everyone wants answered: what happened next?

New methods have also given names to some remains. In 2024, researchers identified a jawbone found on King William Island as belonging to James Fitzjames, captain of Erebus and one of the officers who signed the Victory Point note. Cut marks on the bone provide evidence that his remains were subject to cannibalism. Even a senior officer had not escaped the expedition's terrible fate.

The discovery is a reminder that the mystery is not simply about two ships. It is about individual people, and about the limits of what surviving evidence can tell us. We know Franklin died in June 1847, but not the circumstances of his death. We know the survivors intended to reach the Back River, but their complete route and final timeline remain uncertain.

The search also changed how the story is told. To many people in Britain, the expedition seemed to disappear into a blank space. To Inuit, that space was home. Their ancestors saw evidence of the expedition, shared accounts of it, and passed those accounts on. Those voices were not a footnote to the discovery. They helped make it possible.

Today, Erebus and Terror rest beneath Arctic waters. Their locations are known, and researchers continue to study what they left behind. Yet the distance between a wreck and a full explanation remains vast.

A message says the men would march south. Bones and artifacts mark parts of their journey. Inuit histories preserve encounters and observations. The wrecks offer another record, still being read. Each source brings us closer, but none can speak for every man.

The ships vanished into the ice. The story did not vanish with them. It survived in paper, in objects, and in memories carried across generations. And as long as those clues remain, the search for what happened continues.
