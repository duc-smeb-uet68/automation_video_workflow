This is an original fictional mystery, inspired by historical census records and folklore.

The village counted one hundred and forty-three residents. Clara Venn could find only one hundred and forty-two.

Nobody had vanished. Yet when Clara asked the parish clerk to explain the difference, he carried another chair to the table.

"You'll want that left empty," he said.

Clara had come to Bellwether after the census night of June nineteenth, nineteen twenty-one. Across England and Wales, householders had been asked to record everyone who slept beneath their roof that Sunday. Enumerators collected the completed forms afterward. A visitor counted where the visitor slept. A relative away from home belonged on somebody else's return.

Clara trusted these distinctions. Her brother had died in France. She still laid his letters beside her bed, but she would not put his name on a form that asked who was alive and present. Grief could keep a person close. It could not change an answer.

Bellwether lay beyond a stone bridge between sheep fields. The parish clerk's private tally gave the population as one hundred and forty-three. The household forms contained only one hundred and forty-two names. Clara checked the farms, the cottages, the inn. Nobody claimed another person had slept there on census night.

She expected a missed lodger or an error in addition.

At supper, the innkeeper, Mrs. Bale, served Clara beside the kitchen stove. Her granddaughter May ate opposite. A fourth chair stood between them, with a plate and a folded napkin. Mrs. Bale poured a little milk into a cup and placed it beside the empty plate.

"Are we waiting for someone?" Clara asked.

"Not waiting," Mrs. Bale said. "Leaving room."

That evening Clara visited a farm to check a doubtful age. Four people sat down to supper. Five places were laid. At the next cottage, a widower ate beside an untouched bowl. When Clara reached for its chair, his hand closed around the back before hers could touch it.

"Take mine," he said.

By morning, she had stopped believing the clerk had made a mistake.

He kept his old notebooks beneath the counter. In nineteen eleven, his predecessor had named one hundred and thirty-seven villagers and written a total of one hundred and thirty-eight. In nineteen oh-one, one hundred and fifty-two names became one hundred and fifty-three. Births, funerals, marriages, departures: the village changed. The difference did not.

These were private parish notes, not official census returns. Clara suspected a habit passed between clerks.

"Whom are you adding?" she asked.

The clerk rubbed a pale groove in the counter.

"We don't say."

"Because you don't know?"

His hand stopped moving.

"Because we do."

That night, Clara stayed awake. Shortly after two, a chair scraped across the kitchen floor below her. Then came three small knocks at her bedroom door.

"Mrs. Bale?"

No answer.

Clara opened the door. The passage was empty. On the boards lay a little water, already sinking into the wood. Downstairs, the spare chair had been pulled away from the table. Its plate was clean.

At breakfast, Mrs. Bale blamed May for the noise. May looked up from her bread.

"It wasn't me. I was waiting to see if she'd come upstairs."

Her grandmother set down the kettle so hard that milk trembled in the cups.

The schoolmistress had taught in Bellwether for thirty years. She remembered children who left for the mills and children buried before they learned to write. When Clara mentioned the extra place, the woman drew a register toward herself and held it shut.

"Some things are kinder left alone."

"Kinder to whom?"

Outside, children were singing over a skipping rope. The schoolmistress listened until they stopped. Then she opened a cupboard and gave Clara a bundle of older papers.

Among invoices for coal lay a relief account from eighteen eighty-one: bread, boots, and small payments for village children. One girl appeared again and again. Carrying laundry. Picking stones. Washing the inn steps.

Nell Harrow. Nine years old.

Beside her name, different hands had written different addresses. The mill. The smithy. The inn. Each was crossed out. In the margin, somebody had added: No settled household.

Clara turned the page. The payments stopped in April.

"Where did she go?"

The schoolmistress had arrived long afterward. People told different stories. Nell had run away. Nell had returned to an institution. Nell had never really lived there.

Then the schoolmistress took a small slate from the cupboard. On one corner, beneath later scratches, a child had written her first name. The woman ran her thumb around it without touching the letters.

"Why keep that?" Clara asked.

"I couldn't bear to throw away the last thing that agreed she'd been here."

Mrs. Bale was peeling potatoes when Clara returned. At the name Nell, her knife slipped. A thin strip of skin curled from her thumb. She watched it bleed before reaching for a cloth.

"I was ten," she said. "She used to sleep beside our stove."

Nell had been brought to the village to live with a family who later left. After that, she moved between houses. She worked where she ate. Sometimes she slept where she worked. Nobody wanted the expense of keeping her, and nobody wanted to lose the use of her hands.

On census night in eighteen eighty-one, the innkeeper sent her to the mill. The miller said she belonged at the smithy. The smith's wife gave her bread through a window and told her to go back to the inn.

Rain had been falling since afternoon. By dark, the lower road was underwater.

"My father said she'd gone somewhere else," Mrs. Bale whispered. "I heard her at the kitchen door. I knew his answer wasn't true."

"Did you tell him?"

"He was beside me when she knocked."

Clara looked toward the door. Its lower panel had been replaced with newer wood.

The next morning, Nell's body was found in the millrace. The adults said she must have slipped while crossing in the dark. At the funeral, Mrs. Bale's father explained that the girl had no family in Bellwether. His daughter stood beside him and said nothing.

Then Mrs. Bale explained the chairs. Her mother had laid a place for Nell after the funeral. Soon other houses did the same. At first, people said it was remembrance. Then food began to disappear. Doors knocked in the night. The clerk added a person to his private total, though no household would put down a name.

Over forty years, parents taught children to leave room. They stopped explaining why.

"What happens if you don't?" Clara asked.

"I don't know. I've never dared find out."

That evening, Clara gathered the villagers at the inn. The clerk brought his notebooks; the schoolmistress brought the slate. Mrs. Bale sat beside the stove. May watched the adults arrive.

Clara placed the household forms on the table.

"These record one hundred and forty-two people present on census night," she said. "I won't add a dead child to make your tally right. But I won't carry away another account in which her name is missing."

She set the slate beside the forms.

Nobody spoke. A man near the door stared at the floor. His father had owned the mill.

Then three knocks sounded from the kitchen.

Every face turned toward Mrs. Bale. She pressed both hands against her knees. May reached past her and lifted the latch.

The door opened onto darkness. Cool air crossed the room. There was no one on the step.

May pulled out her own chair.

"You can have this one," she said. "It's nearer the fire."

Clara could not afterward explain why those words frightened her more than the knocking. Perhaps because nobody had told May what to say. Perhaps because the girl waited with the patience children give to other children who are shy.

Mrs. Bale began to cry. She looked at the doorway rather than the empty place.

"Nell Harrow," she said. "You came back to our door. I heard you."

The clerk tried to interrupt. She raised a hand.

"I heard you," she said again.

One by one, the others spoke. Some knew only what their parents had admitted. Some had pieces of the story Clara had not found: a borrowed coat returned wet, a pair of boots sold after the funeral, a small unpaid wage. Clara wrote them down. Nobody could make Nell's last walk harmless by changing a total now.

The stove settled with a soft click. May climbed into her grandmother's lap. Her own chair remained pulled out beside the warmth.

When Clara went upstairs, she passed the kitchen door. Across the dry flags ran a trail of small wet marks. They led from the threshold toward the table. She fetched the lamp for a closer look, but the water had already begun to fade.

She left Bellwether two days later. The official forms still contained one hundred and forty-two names. In the clerk's private book, below the corrected total, was a new account of Nell Harrow's life and death. Mrs. Bale had signed it. Others were beginning to add their names.

Before Clara crossed the bridge, May ran after her with the gloves she had left behind.

"Grandmother says we can put the spare chairs away."

"Will you?"

May considered it.

"I think we'll keep one. Someone might need to sit down."

Bellwether had counted an extra resident without giving her a home. Clara could not prove who knocked or left water on a dry floor. But she had found the child in their own handwriting, carrying laundry between houses that called her somebody else's responsibility.

On the far side of the bridge, Clara stopped to put on her gloves. Then she took out her notebook. Beneath Nell's name, she added the small thing May had told her that morning.

For the first time she could remember, her grandmother had slept through the night.
